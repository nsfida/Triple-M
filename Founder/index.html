The Person Behind Triplem VIP

A life shaped by systems, ideas, observation and the urge to build.

My work moves between business, technology and creative practice, but the instinct underneath it is remarkably consistent: understand what is happening, remove unnecessary friction, and leave something clearer than I found it.

I am Nadeem Shahzad Fida, originally from Khawrai, Pakistan and now based in Dubai, United Arab Emirates. In my everyday professional life I work as an Operations Manager at Premium Car Rentals DMCC. Outside the office, I am the founder of Triplem VIP, an author, a FIDE-rated chess player, a photographer, a licensed UAE drone pilot and someone who keeps building small and large digital ideas whenever a real problem gives me a reason to.

I do not see these as unrelated identities. To me, operations is about understanding movement; accounting is about understanding consequence; software is about translating rules into a usable system; chess is about choosing well under uncertainty; photography is about deciding what deserves attention; and writing is about giving thought a durable form. They are different languages for the same instinct: look closely, think clearly, and build deliberately.

My professional path was not designed as a straight line. I began by teaching Mathematics and Physics, later taught Accounting and Business Mathematics, moved into industrial data coordination in a manufacturing environment, then into accounts in Dubai, and eventually into operations leadership. Each chapter added a different kind of discipline. When I later began building financial software, I was not approaching money management as an abstract programming exercise. I had already spent years seeing what records, transactions, delays, reconciliations, customers and operational decisions look like in real life.

That is an important part of the story behind Triplem VIP. I wanted a system that felt less like a piece of accounting software and more like a private command centre for everyday financial life. Something that could hold the practical mess of wallets, loans, inventory, assets, receipts, invoices and obligations without making the person using it feel buried under the machinery.

There is also a quieter side to me that does not appear in business titles. I like the concentration of a chess position, the stillness before a photograph, the solitude of writing, and the peculiar satisfaction of turning an untidy idea into an interface that suddenly makes sense. Much of my work begins there, in the space between curiosity and order.

The Road Here

The work changed. The habit of thinking did not.

Instead of listing job titles, this is the part of my story that explains what each chapter left behind and why those lessons eventually found their way into Triplem VIP.

I have worked in classrooms, on the data side of a manufacturing floor, in accounts, and in day-to-day operations. Those environments look very different from the outside. From the inside, however, all of them depend on the same things: information arriving correctly, people understanding what to do next, small errors being noticed before they become expensive, and systems being simple enough to survive real life.

01
The classroom

Teaching taught me that clarity is a responsibility.

In 2015 and 2016, I taught Mathematics and Physics at Roshan Education Center in Jehangira. During 2016 and 2017, I also taught Mathematics, Accounting and Business Mathematics at Ideal Academy in Baldia Town, Karachi. Teaching numerical subjects is unforgiving: if the explanation is vague, the learner cannot hide from it for long. You have to break a difficult idea into the right sequence and make every step earn its place.

That habit stayed with me. When I design a screen today, I still think like a teacher. What does the person need to understand first? What can wait? Which word creates confusion? Which number actually matters? Good software, like good teaching, does not impress people with complexity. It gives them the confidence to move forward.

02
The factory floor

Industry taught me to respect data before making decisions from it.

From 2016 to 2018 I worked full time as a Data Coordinator in the Spinning Department at Gatron Industries Limited in Hub, Balochistan. Manufacturing gives data a very physical meaning. A figure is not merely a cell in a spreadsheet; it can represent output, movement, material, timing or something that an entire chain of people will use to make the next decision.

That environment strengthened my respect for structured records and consistency. It also showed me something that later became central to my own software work: data is only useful when it remains understandable. A system can collect thousands of entries and still fail its users if the information cannot be retrieved, compared or trusted when it matters.

03
The books and ledgers

Accounts taught me how far one small number can travel.

When I joined Premium Car Rentals DMCC in Dubai in 2018, my earlier role was on the accounts side of the business as an Accounts Executive. Working close to transactions, balances, reconciliations, receivables, payables and customer records changed the way I thought about financial information. A missing entry is rarely just a missing entry. It can change a balance, delay a decision, complicate a conversation or make the next person spend time reconstructing what should already be clear.

This is where much of the emotional logic behind Triplem VIP comes from. Financial control should not require remembering which spreadsheet held which figure or why a balance no longer agrees. The record should tell its own story.

04
The operational view

Operations taught me to stop looking at one department at a time.

I later progressed within Premium Car Rentals DMCC into my current role as Operations Manager. Operations changes the scale of the question. Instead of asking whether one transaction is correct, you begin asking whether the wider flow is working: how people, vehicles, customer needs, records, finance and daily decisions connect, and where a small delay can quietly become a larger problem.

That wider view influences how I build products. I am interested in the connections between modules, not merely the modules themselves. An expense belongs to a wallet. An asset has a cost history. Inventory affects profit. A loan changes obligations. A report should emerge from the same underlying truth rather than being rebuilt separately. Triplem VIP is, in many ways, my attempt to give personal and small-business finance an operational view.

Why I Built Triplem VIP

It started with a simple frustration: financial life becomes messy long before it becomes large.

Triplem VIP is my flagship private accounting and finance platform. I built it from the perspective of someone who has worked with accounts, records and operations, not from the assumption that every user wants to become an accountant before they can understand their own money.

Everyday finance is fragmented. Cash sits in one place, bank and card spending in another. Loans are remembered in messages. Inventory lives in a sheet. Asset expenses are scattered through receipts. Installments have dates of their own. A small business may understand each piece separately and still struggle to see the complete position. Triplem VIP exists to bring those pieces into one calm, searchable workspace.

The platform connects expense wallets, loans, inventory, invoices, receipts, asset management, installments, notes, multi-currency records, reports and browser-controlled Bitcoin tools. The point is not to add features for the sake of a longer menu. The point is that these things already coexist in real financial life, so the software should understand their relationship too.

I am especially deliberate about the feeling of control. A user should be able to look back and understand what happened without reconstructing the past from memory. Records should be exportable. Reports should be readable. Search should be useful. Sensitive areas should not be designed carelessly. With Bitcoin tools, for example, private-key handling is kept on the client side rather than being treated as ordinary server data.

Triplem VIP also reflects a broader design belief of mine: powerful software does not need to look intimidating. The interface should carry the complexity so the person does not have to. That is why the landing experience, dashboard, reports and individual modules are designed around clarity, visual hierarchy, responsive access and practical speed.

A move may look attractive and still be wrong; a quiet move may seem ordinary and change the entire position. Over time, the board became less a hobby and more a private laboratory for attention, patience and decision-making.

The influence reaches into my working life almost without permission. Operations often feels positional: a problem appearing in one place may have been created several moves earlier. Product design feels similar: adding something is easy, but understanding what it weakens elsewhere is harder. Chess trained me to look for those second-order effects. It also taught me that patience is not inactivity; it is disciplined preparation for the right moment to act.

Calculation: Seeing multiple moves ahead, on the board and in business, before committing to a line.
Positional Thinking: Small, consistent advantages compound over time. No single move wins a game; patterns do.
Time Pressure: Every second on the clock is finite. Decision-making under pressure is a skill, not a gift.
Analysis: After every game, win or lose, the position is reviewed. Failure is only permanent if you refuse to study it.
Through the Lens

Photography is how I practise noticing what would otherwise disappear.

I do not describe myself as a professional photographer. I photograph because the act of framing the world changes the way I see it, whether the camera is on the ground or in the air.

I came to photography without the intention of turning it into a profession. I simply enjoyed the discipline of looking long enough for an ordinary scene to become interesting. The camera asks a surprisingly difficult question: what, in this entire field of view, actually deserves to stay?

That question is why photography became more than a casual interest. A photograph is a decision about time: this moment existed, this angle mattered, this light will not return in exactly the same way. I am drawn to images that still feel truthful after the novelty of the scene has gone.

My subjects range from landscapes in Pakistan and the UAE to architecture, streets, nature and the quiet geometry of everyday life. I also hold UAE drone pilot certification, which gave me another way to understand familiar places from unfamiliar height. One of my photographs, Turquoise Boundary, was selected for America's Favorite Photos and progressed to the Quarter Finals. That recognition was meaningful precisely because photography began for me without professional pretension.

Photography has also influenced the way I design interfaces. Framing is not only about what you include; it is about what you have the confidence to leave outside the frame. A crowded screen and a crowded photograph can fail for the same reason. Clarity is not emptiness. It is choosing what deserves attention.

The Written Side

Neerang-e-Hikmat is where questions became pages.

Neerang-e-Hikmat · نیرنگِ حکمت

Neerang-e-Hikmat is my published Urdu work, but I think of it less as a collection of answers and more as a record of questions I refused to leave shallow. It moves through Islamic reflection, philosophy, spirituality, the mystery of existence, human purpose, love and the relationship between the created being and the Creator.

The published edition contains more than 170 pages and is listed by PaperCraft Publishers under ISBN 9786277771232. Its themes include the Oneness of Allah, the universe and cosmological wonder, the purpose of human creation, true and worldly love, and reflective poetry. I wanted the book to move between intellect and feeling rather than treating them as enemies.

Writing it required a different kind of patience from software. Code tells you quickly when something breaks. A paragraph can be grammatically correct and still be untrue to what you meant. A poem can have beautiful language and still lack weight. The process taught me to distrust the first convenient sentence and keep searching for the more precise one.

For me, writing completes the same circle as the rest of my work. Chess asks what follows from a move. Photography asks what deserves attention. Software asks what can be made clearer. Writing asks what is worth preserving once the moment has passed.

What Connects It All

Different interests, the same recurring questions.

I do not think of these as a list of skills. They are patterns that keep reappearing in the way I work, build, write and make decisions.
01

Can this be clearer?

Clarity is probably the strongest link between my teaching background and my product work. Whether I am explaining a mathematical idea, reviewing an operational process or designing a finance screen, I keep asking what can be removed, renamed, regrouped or made more obvious without losing substance.

02

What is the real consequence?

Numbers are never just numbers. A balance affects a decision. A delay affects another person. A chess move changes the next position. I tend to think in consequences and connections rather than isolated actions.

03

What are we not seeing?

Photography trained my attention; operations trained my peripheral vision. Both taught me that the obvious subject is not always the important one. Sometimes the problem is outside the frame everyone is staring at.

04

Does the user retain control?

I prefer tools that respect the person using them. That means understandable records, exportable data, privacy-conscious design and fewer situations in which the software knows what is happening but the user does not.

05

Is it useful after the excitement fades?

I like new technology, but novelty is not the same thing as usefulness. The products and community tools I care about are the ones that still make sense on an ordinary Tuesday when nobody is impressed by the technology anymore. That is when design becomes service.
